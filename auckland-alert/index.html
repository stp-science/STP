The evidence collection
File A · The setting

Two plates. Different volcanic stories.

New Zealand straddles the Australian and Pacific plates. These are moving slabs of solid lithosphere: the crust and the rigid uppermost mantle.

Auckland lies on the Australian Plate, west of the present plate boundary. In the eastern North Island, the Pacific Plate descends beneath the Australian Plate. This process is subduction.

Water released from the descending plate helps rock in the mantle above it partially melt. This helps explain volcanism in the Taupō Volcanic Zone. Auckland's volcanic field is a different setting: it lies within the plate, away from the boundary.

Boundary reminder: plates can converge (move together), diverge (move apart), or slide past each other. Volcanoes are common at subduction zones and divergent boundaries. Sliding past mainly produces earthquakes.

Sources: Te Ara: plate-boundary map; Earth Sciences NZ: geology of New Zealand; Research on Auckland's tectonic setting.

File B · Beneath the surface

The mantle can supply magma within a plate.

Intraplate means “within a plate”. Auckland is an example of intraplate volcanism. A volcano does not always need a plate boundary above its magma source.

Deep beneath Auckland, a small proportion of mantle rock melts. This is partial melting. The mantle is mostly solid rock, not an underground ocean of liquid magma.

Evidence points to a magma source roughly 70–90 km deep. Small batches of magma can separate from this source and rise. Magma is less dense than surrounding solid rock, and pressure helps it force pathways through fractures in the rock above. If it reaches the surface, it erupts. Magma at the surface is called lava.

You may see Auckland's source called a “hotspot”. This is useful shorthand, but the exact cause of melting remains under investigation. We should not assume Auckland has an identical deep mantle plume to Hawaii.

Sources: GeoNet: magma source depth; Hopkins and colleagues: Auckland Volcanic Field research review.

File C · A field, not one mountain

A new route can create a new volcano.

Auckland has more than 50 recognised volcanic centres. A volcanic field contains many vents spread across an area, rather than one large volcano supplied through the same vent again and again.

Most Auckland centres formed during one episode of activity. Later batches of magma usually found different routes through the crust and formed new vents elsewhere. An existing cone is therefore unlikely to erupt again, while the field can still produce a new volcano.

Rangitoto is an exception to a simple “one eruption per volcano” rule: evidence indicates more than one eruptive episode. “Unlikely” is more accurate than “impossible”.

Source: GeoNet volcano FAQs, Auckland section.

File D · Reading a quiet landscape

Quiet does not mean extinct.

The most recent eruption formed Rangitoto around 600 years ago. Eruptions have occurred at irregular intervals over a much longer history. A human lifetime without an eruption is a short part of that history.

Scientists regard the Auckland Volcanic Field as still active: it retains the potential to generate future eruptions. That describes its long-term capability, not a claim that an eruption is happening or about to happen.

A quiet old cone and a potentially active field can exist at the same time. Keep those two scales separate when you explain Auckland's risk.

What can we say?
A future eruption is possible. A long quiet interval does not show that the magma-generating system has permanently stopped.

Past eruption sites do not give a dependable sequence for locating the next vent. Scientists cannot currently name its exact place or date in advance. Monitoring may detect signs of unrest and help assess a developing situation.

Preparation matters because a new vent could form close to homes, roads or other infrastructure. Uncertainty is a reason to plan carefully.

Sources: GeoNet: eruption history and continuing activity; GeoNet: uncertainty about new vents.

Useful vocabulary

Intraplate

Within a tectonic plate, away from its boundary.

Partial melting

Only part of a rock melts. The rest remains solid.

Magma / lava

Magma is molten rock below the surface. Lava is molten rock that has erupted at the surface.

Vent

An opening where volcanic material reaches the surface.

Volcanic field

An area containing many volcanic vents or centres.

Extinct

A volcano considered unlikely to erupt again because its supply system has ceased. Auckland's field as a whole is not classified this way.
